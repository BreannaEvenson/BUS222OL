01 / Getting oriented

Meet the securities market

A security is a type of investment. Securities markets connect organizations seeking capital with people who want to invest. There are two important markets to distinguish.

Primary market

New securities are sold by the issuer to investors. The issuer receives money to fund operations or growth.

Secondary market

Already-issued securities change hands between investors. The issuer generally does not receive money from that resale.

02 / Identifying investments

What counts as a security?

Federal law names familiar instruments such as stocks, bonds, and notes, but also includes less obvious investment arrangements. Courts look at the economic reality of an arrangement, not just its label.

The Howey frameworkIn general, an investment contract involves an investment of money or other value in a common enterprise with an expectation of profit based primarily on the efforts of others. The details matter, and a particular arrangement requires legal analysis.

Less obvious examples

  • Crowdfunding: Donating to a cause is different from purchasing an investment interest; the structure of the offer matters.
  • Promissory notes: A note may be a security, subject to the family resemblance analysis discussed in Reves v. Ernst & Young.
  • Digital assets: Whether a transaction involving a crypto asset is regulated as a security depends on its particular facts and applicable law.
  • Virtual stock-market games: A simulated game is different from an arrangement that takes real investor money and promises returns.

03 / Types of investments

Equity vs. debt

Businesses can raise capital by selling ownership interests or by borrowing money. Those approaches create different rights and expectations for investors.

Equity = ownership

Common stock: ownership interest; dividends are not guaranteed.

Preferred stock: usually carries certain priority rights, including dividend preferences, over common shares.

Debt = borrowing

Bonds, notes, and debentures: represent amounts the issuer owes; payments and repayment terms are described in the debt agreement.

Peer-to-peer lending: platforms can connect borrowers and lenders.

Think like a business ownerIf a business issues shares, it may give investors an ownership stake. If it issues bonds, it takes on an obligation to repay under agreed terms.

04 / Raising capital legally

Issuing securities: disclosure first

The central idea behind securities regulation is disclosure: investors should have access to important, truthful information before making decisions.

Securities Act of 1933

Primarily addresses offers and sales of securities by issuers, including registration and disclosures, unless an exemption applies.

SEC and EDGAR

The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) administers and enforces federal securities laws. EDGAR is its public database of company filings.

A simplified public-offering sequence

1. PrepareGather financial information and offering documents, including a prospectus.
2. File and waitFile the registration statement with the SEC; restrictions apply while the registration statement is under review.
3. Effective offeringOnce the registration statement becomes effective, the issuer can proceed with sales under applicable rules.
Exempt does not mean unregulatedSome offerings, including qualifying private placements and certain crowdfunding offerings, may rely on registration exemptions. Antifraud rules and applicable disclosure requirements can still apply.

05 / Transactions and accountability

Trading, disclosure, and fraud

The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 establishes major rules governing securities markets, ongoing public-company reporting, and securities fraud. It also provides a framework for SEC oversight of exchanges and market participants.

Rule 10b-5

Prohibits fraud and deceptive conduct in connection with purchasing or selling securities, including material misstatements or omissions under applicable standards.

Insider trading

Trading on material, nonpublic information can be unlawful when it violates a duty of trust or confidence or other applicable rules.

Tipper and tippee

Passing along confidential information may trigger liability for the source and, in some circumstances, the recipient who trades.

Section 16

Requires certain officers, directors, and more-than-10% beneficial owners to report holdings and trades; short-swing profit recovery rules may apply.

06 / Corporate and market oversight

When regulation goes beyond the trade

Securities law also shapes how public corporations report finances, how auditors work, and how the financial system handles risk.

Sarbanes–Oxley Act

Strengthened public-company auditing, financial reporting, and corporate-governance requirements. It created the PCAOB, includes officer-certification and audit-committee provisions, and contains whistleblower protections.

TARP

The Troubled Asset Relief Program was established in response to the financial crisis to help stabilize the financial system, including through assistance to financial institutions. Recipients faced conditions and oversight.

Dodd–Frank Act

The Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act expanded regulation and oversight after the financial crisis, including creation of the Financial Stability Oversight Council and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau.

Whistleblowers and accountability

Financial reporting, controls, oversight, and lawful reporting of suspected misconduct help protect investors and confidence in markets.
