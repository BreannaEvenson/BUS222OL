Contract Enforceability, Performance, Breach & Remedies

A contract can contain the basic elements needed for formation and still raise questions about whether it is enforceable, whether the parties properly performed, whether a breach occurred, and what remedy is available.

Work through each section below. You will encounter short explanations, examples, and knowledge checks as you move through the material.

Enforceability Conditions Performance & Discharge Breach Remedies Mitigation Third Parties

1. Enforceability

Even if the elements of a contract are present, the agreement must still be the product of genuine assent and, in certain circumstances, must be in writing in order to be enforceable.

Genuine Assent

Genuine assent means the parties truly agreed to the terms of the contract without improper conduct such as misrepresentation, duress, undue influence, or unconscionability.

MISREPRESENTATION

A contract may be avoided when one party makes a false representation about a material fact and the other party justifiably relies on that statement.

FRAUDULENT MISREPRESENTATION

Includes the elements of misrepresentation plus actual knowledge that the represented fact is false.

CONCEALMENT

Misrepresentation may also arise when a party uses a half-truth, actively conceals the truth, or fails to correct a statement later discovered to be false.

DURESS

Unfair coercion is used to induce another party to enter into or modify a contract.

UNDUE INFLUENCE

Improper pressure is placed on a person through a trusted relationship.

UNCONSCIONABILITY

A narrowly applied defense involving a grossly unfair burden that shocks the objective conscience.

Statute of Frauds

When Does a Contract Need to Be in Writing?

The Statute of Frauds determines which contracts must be written in order to be enforceable.

Common Law Contracts

The writing requirement applies to contracts involving:

  • Sale of land
  • Contracts that cannot be performed in under one year
  • Promises to pay the debt of another
  • Contracts made in consideration of marriage

UCC Contracts

  • Sale of goods for $500 or more
  • Lease of goods totaling $1,000 or more

E-mails may satisfy a writing requirement under some circumstances, although treatment varies by state.

Interpreting Written Contracts

Parole Evidence Rule

A writing intended as the final expression of the parties' agreement generally cannot be contradicted by oral or written agreements made before the final writing.

Ambiguous or Missing Terms

Ambiguous language may be construed against the party who drafted it. Courts may also supply a reasonable term where the contract is silent.

2. Nature and Effect of Conditions

A condition is an event that triggers a particular performance and helps allocate the risk that part of the contract may not be completed.

Condition Precedent

An event that must occur before performance becomes due.

Condition Subsequent

An event that occurs after performance and can affect an existing obligation.

Concurrent Conditions

Both parties are required to render performance simultaneously.

3. Good Faith Performance & Discharge

When both parties faithfully complete their promised performances in good faith, their contractual duties are discharged.

Substantial Performance

If a party makes a good-faith effort and substantially performs the essential duties required by the contract, a court may enforce the agreement.

The innocent party may still recover damages for the imperfect portion of the performance.

Other Ways Contract Duties Can Be Discharged

Mutual Consent

Rescission
If neither party has fully performed, they may mutually agree to cancel the contract.
Accord and Satisfaction
One party promises a substitute future performance (accord) and the other agrees to accept it in satisfaction of the existing obligation.
Modification
The original agreement is replaced with a substitute agreement.
Novation
The parties agree to substitute a third party for one of the original contracting parties.

Operation of Law

Impossibility
No one can objectively do it. Examples may include destruction of the subject matter, death or incapacity, failure of the contemplated means of performance, or performance becoming illegal.
Impracticability
I can't do it. An unforeseeable and extreme burden, particularly in terms of cost, may support discharge.
Frustration of Purpose
Why would I want to do it now? The main purpose of the agreement becomes substantially frustrated by an event that both parties assumed would not occur.
Other Operation-of-Law Events
Discharge may also occur because of unilateral alteration, bankruptcy relief, or expiration of the statute of limitations.

4. Breach of Contract & Anticipatory Repudiation

Total Breach

One party fails to perform its duties substantially enough that the nonbreaching party may suspend performance, be discharged, and recover damages.

Partial Breach

A failure to perform that is not substantial enough to discharge the other party, although damages may still be available.

Anticipatory Repudiation

Before performance is due, one party clearly communicates or demonstrates that performance will not occur.

5. Remedies for Breach

Remedies are designed to address the harm caused by breach. Courts may award money damages or, when money is inadequate, provide an equitable remedy.

Remedies at Law: Monetary Damages

Compensatory Damages

Money awarded for the direct loss caused by nonperformance.

Consequential Damages

Money awarded for foreseeable indirect losses resulting from the breach.

Restitution

The nonbreaching party may rescind and recover the fair market value of services already rendered when the other party materially breaches.

Liquidated Damages

Damages agreed upon by the parties ahead of time in the contract.

Hadley v. Baxendale

Consequential damages are limited to losses that can reasonably be considered a direct, natural, and foreseeable consequence of the breach and within the contemplation of the parties when the contract was made.

Equitable Remedies

Specific Performance

A court orders the breaching party to perform the promised action.

Used when the subject matter is sufficiently unique and money damages are inadequate.

Injunctive Relief

A court orders a party to refrain from performing a particular act.

Reformation

The court rewrites the contract so that it reflects the parties' actual intentions when the original agreement imperfectly expressed them.

6. Avoidance & Mitigation of Damages

Duty to Mitigate

The nonbreaching party must make reasonable efforts to avoid excessive or unnecessary losses. Damages that reasonably could have been avoided may not be recoverable.

In the employment context, an employee claiming breach generally has a duty to seek available replacement employment.

Clean Hands & Tender of Performance

A party seeking relief for breach must come to court with clean hands, meaning the party was not responsible for the breach.

Tender of performance means the party was ready, willing, and able to perform its own obligations.

Key idea: A party generally cannot allow avoidable damages to grow unnecessarily and then expect the breaching party to pay for all of them.

7. Contracts Involving Third-Party Rights

Contract rights or duties may sometimes involve someone who was not one of the original contracting parties.

Assignment

The unilateral transfer of contractual rights from one party to a third party.

Assignment may be limited by an antiassignment clause, a material change in burden, or legal prohibition.

Delegation

The transfer of contractual duties to a third party.

The original party remains liable for nonperformance. Delegation may be prohibited when special personal skills are required, a nondelegation clause exists, or the delegatee is a competitor of the nondelegating party.

Third-Party Beneficiary

A third person benefits from a promise between two other contracting parties.

An intended beneficiary may seek damages, while an incidental beneficiary generally may not.

Chapter Summary

  • A contract may be unenforceable when genuine assent is undermined by misrepresentation, duress, undue influence, or unconscionability.
  • The Statute of Frauds requires certain agreements to be written.
  • Conditions determine when contractual performance becomes due or is affected.
  • Good-faith completion of performance normally discharges contractual obligations.
  • Duties may also be discharged by agreement of the parties or by operation of law.
  • A breach may be total, partial, or anticipated before performance is due.
  • Remedies may include compensatory, consequential, restitution, or liquidated damages, as well as equitable remedies such as specific performance, injunction, or reformation.
  • A nonbreaching party has a duty to take reasonable steps to mitigate avoidable damages.
  • Contractual rights and duties may sometimes involve third parties through assignment, delegation, or third-party beneficiary status.
