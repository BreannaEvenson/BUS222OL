Overview

This chapter begins with a very brief history and description of the structure of the U.S. Constitution with a focus on the first three articles and the powers of Congress (especially the Commerce Clause). As a practical matter, it may be best to assume that students have only a very basic knowledge of the purpose, history or challenges associated with having a written constitution. Making the leap between understanding the nature of the constitution and how it impacts business entities can be a significant hurdle for students. Therefore, the chapter first lays out the nuts and bolts, then covers black letter law, and concludes with applications and impact of the constitutional principles in a business context.

The United States uses a federal system in which a national government with limited regulatory powers granted by the Constitution coexists with the government of each state. The Constitution establishes the structure of the federal government and the rules for amending the document, grants specific powers to the different branches of government, and provides procedural protections for U.S. citizens against wrongful government action.

The Constitution is composed of a preamble, seven articles, and 27 amendments. The preamble states the Constitution's broad objectives, while the articles establish governmental structure, powers, and procedures. The Constitution also establishes boundaries of jurisdiction. Amendments are additions or changes to the Constitution, and the first 10 amendments make up the Bill of Rights.

Applying the Constitution: Standards of Review

When reviewing a government action for constitutional soundness, the Court classifies the action into one of three categories of scrutiny: rational basis review, intermediate-level scrutiny, or strict scrutiny.

1
Rational Basis: The government need only show that its action advanced a legitimate government objective and that the action was minimally related to that objective.
2
Intermediate-Level Scrutiny: The government must prove that its action advanced an important government objective and that the action is substantially related to that objective.
3
Strict Scrutiny: The government's objective must be compelling; the means chosen must be necessary to achieve that compelling objective; and no less-restrictive alternative can exist.
The Supremacy Clause and Preemption: Under the Supremacy Clause, federal laws preempt, or override, conflicting state laws.

The Commerce Clause

Congress's broadest power is derived from the Commerce Clause, which gives Congress authority to regulate commerce among the states. This authority gives Congress broad power over people, products, transactions, and activities sufficiently connected to interstate commerce.

Congress may regulate the channels of interstate commerce, the instrumentalities used in interstate commerce, articles moving through interstate commerce, and intrastate activity that has a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce. The Supreme Court has even permitted federal regulation of a product cultivated for noncommercial purposes entirely within one state when the activity was considered sufficiently related to interstate commerce, as illustrated by Gonzales v. Raich.

The Commerce Clause and Civil Rights

Congress has also used its commerce power to support civil-rights legislation. In the Civil Rights Act of 1964, Congress relied on the Commerce Clause to ban discrimination in places of public accommodation, including privately owned restaurants and hotels. Cases such as Katzenbach v. McClung and Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States illustrate this use of federal commerce power.

Noncommercial Activity

Congressional commerce authority is not unlimited. When an activity is primarily noncommercial, the activity Congress seeks to regulate must have a sufficient connection to a legitimate economic interest. Cases such as United States v. Lopez and United States v. Morrison illustrate limits on Congress's commerce power.

State Regulation and the Dormant Commerce Clause

States may regulate commerce that crosses their borders when the regulation is a legitimate effort to protect health, safety, and welfare and does not discriminate against interstate commerce. The Dormant Commerce Clause reflects the principle that states generally may not discriminate against or place an undue burden on interstate commerce.

Tax and Spend Power

Congress has the power to tax the citizenry and spend federal funds in ways that promote the common defense and general welfare.

Under the Necessary and Proper Clause, Congress may also attach conditions to the use of federal money in order to accomplish public-policy objectives, as illustrated by South Dakota v. Dole.

Constitutional Protections

The Bill of Rights contains protections for citizens from unlawful or repressive acts by government and guarantees rights of due process. Corporations and other business entities, however, do not always receive the same level of constitutional protection as individuals.

The First Amendment

The First Amendment begins with the important phrase “Congress shall make no law” and protects religion, press, speech, assembly, and the right to petition the government.

Limits on Free Speech

First Amendment protection is broad but not absolute. Government may impose reasonable restrictions related to the time, place, and manner of expression in appropriate circumstances.

Commercial Speech

Commercial speech, especially advertising, receives partial First Amendment protection when it concerns lawful activity and is truthful. Virginia State Board of Pharmacy v. Virginia Citizens Consumer Council helped establish constitutional protection for truthful commercial advertising.

Central Hudson Gas v. Public Service Commission established a four-part framework for evaluating government restrictions on commercial speech:

  1. The speech must concern lawful activity and not be misleading.
  2. The government must have a substantial interest in regulating it.
  3. The restriction must directly advance the government's asserted interest.
  4. The restriction must be no more extensive than necessary to achieve that interest.
Advertising and Obscenity

Government regulation of potentially offensive or obscene commercial advertising is subject to constitutional scrutiny, as illustrated by Bad Frog Brewery, Inc. v. New York State Liquor Authority.

Corporate Political Speech

Political speech by corporations generally receives strong First Amendment protection. In Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission, the Supreme Court held that government may not prohibit all independent political spending by corporations in candidate elections.

The Fourth Amendment

Basic requirement: A warrantless search or seizure by a government actor is generally unlawful unless an exception applies.

Common Exceptions
  • Exigent circumstances
  • Consensual searches
  • Searches incident to arrest
  • Plain-view doctrine
  • Terry stops
Expectation of Privacy

Fourth Amendment protection generally depends on whether a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy. Items knowingly exposed to the public are generally not protected in the same way as one's body, clothing, or personal belongings. Public records and observable personal characteristics generally carry less expectation of privacy.

Reasonableness

Courts evaluate Fourth Amendment reasonableness using the totality of the circumstances.

Searches and Seizures
  • Physical searches: May include traditional searches as well as certain dog sniffs or electronic surveillance.
  • Seizure of a person: Occurs when government detains a person so that the person is not free to leave.
  • Seizure of property: Occurs when government meaningfully interferes with a person's possessory interest in property.
  • Electronic searches: Technology-based surveillance can raise Fourth Amendment issues.
Privacy and the USA Patriot Act

The USA Patriot Act, enacted after the September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks, expanded government authority in certain terrorism-related investigations involving financial records, communications, and Internet activity.

Although the Constitution does not expressly state a general right to privacy, Supreme Court decisions have recognized privacy interests derived from several constitutional protections. Congress has also enacted statutory privacy protections governing particular types of information.

Workplace Privacy

Many constitutional privacy protections restrict government action and therefore do not automatically apply to private workplaces. However, federal and state statutes increasingly shape privacy obligations for employers and employees.

Due Process

The Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments protect individuals from being deprived of “life, liberty, or property” without due process of law.

The Fourteenth Amendment makes many protections contained in the Bill of Rights applicable to state governments.

Due process serves two major purposes. First, it imposes procedural requirements on federal and state governments before they may deprive a person of protected interests. Second, it limits the substantive power of government to regulate certain areas involving individual liberties.

Equal Protection: The Equal Protection Clause generally requires government to treat people who are similarly situated alike.
