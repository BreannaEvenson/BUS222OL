What Is Ethics?

Ethics is the set of moral principles or core values used for deciding between right and wrong. As a result, ethics applies to our everyday decisions.

Relationship Between Law and Ethics

A comprehensive understanding of law and ethics first starts with the overarching notion of justice. Broadly defined, justice is the maintenance or administration of what is fair. As an ideal, the law strives to encode basic notions of justice and fairness. In an ideal system, the law should always strive to achieve justice and produce the right outcome.

Business ethics is a consciousness of what is right or wrong in the workplace and taking responsibility for an ethical course of action in business operations given its impact on owners, investors, employees, customers, and the community at large. Collectively, these groups are known as stakeholders.

Primary stakeholders generally experience a direct impact from a business decision, while secondary stakeholders may not have a direct connection to the business but can still experience indirect consequences.

Morals refer to generally accepted standards of right and wrong in a given society, while ethics describes the use of a conscious system for deciding moral dilemmas.

Principles-Based Approach

Ethical decisions are made according to a set of established principles or standards that guide individual decision making. These principles may come from sources such as religious teachings, philosophical beliefs, or established ethical codes.

Aristotle: Virtue ethics focuses on whether our actions contribute to the development of good moral character.
Kant: The categorical imperative asks us to consider whether our decision would still be acceptable if everyone in society acted the same way.

Consequences-Based Approach

This approach comes from the utilitarian tradition of moral philosophy. It evaluates a decision primarily by its consequences.

The most ethical course of action is the one that creates the greatest benefit for the greatest number of people while limiting harmful consequences.

Moral Philosophy and Ethical Decision Making

Where do morals come from? There are many possible sources, including family, church, school, community norms, personal experiences, and moral reasoning.

Principles-Based Approach

This approach evaluates decisions according to guiding principles. Those principles may come from religion, where ethical behavior is guided by religious tenets; from virtue ethics, which asks whether conduct develops good moral character; or from natural law, which holds that some rights and moral values are universal and timeless.

Kant's categorical imperative adds the idea that a person should act morally because it is the right thing to do and should consider what would happen if everyone followed the same rule.

Consequences-Based Approach

This approach focuses on the outcome of the decision. The ethical course of action is the one that provides the greatest good or happiness for the greatest number of people while creating the least harmful consequences for the majority of the community.

Contract-Based Approach

This approach views ethical rules as something people would reasonably agree to together. Individuals negotiate standards while imagining that they do not know what position they themselves will occupy in society.

This idea is sometimes described as making decisions behind a veil of ignorance, which encourages people to create rules that remain fair regardless of who benefits from them.

Values Management

Challenges to Business Ethics

Values management is the prioritizing of moral values for the organization and ensuring that organizational behavior remains aligned with those values. Yet, despite evidence that embracing business ethics can support profitability and sound public policy, resistance to formal values management programs still exists in some business cultures.

Strategic Advantages

Managing ethics in the workplace involves identifying and prioritizing values that guide behavior, then developing policies and procedures that support those expectations. Rather than treating ethics as separate from business strategy, values management can be given the same level of importance as other management priorities.

Organizations must decide whether they are simply trying to meet a moral minimum—essentially doing no harm or as little harm as possible—or whether their values will play a larger role in business decisions. Strong values management can improve teamwork and productivity, create greater clarity in business operations, strengthen the organization's public image, and help leaders stay committed to an ethical course during difficult or turbulent periods.

Typically, ethics programs convey corporate values, often using codes and policies to guide decisions and behavior, and can include extensive training and evaluation depending on the organizational structure.

Corporate Social Responsibility

Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) involves a broader-based identification of important business and social issues and a critique of business organizations and practices. The underlying idea is that conscience resides not only in individuals, but also in the corporation itself.

Three Views of Corporate Social Responsibility

Read each description and select the CSR viewpoint that best matches it.

“Greed is good.”

The only responsibility a business has is to maximize shareholder wealth because what is good for business is good for society's well-being. Market efficiencies and the invisible hand are expected to guide morality and responsibility.

“Just follow the law.”

Corporations have a responsibility to pursue objectives that are rational, legal, and purely economic. Law, regulation, and the political process provide the primary basis for responsible business decision making.

“Good corporate citizenship.”

Corporations have a primary social responsibility, with profitability treated as secondary. Under this view, organizations have an obligation to take a meaningful role in promoting social responsibility.

The Broad View

Under the broad view, CSR is considered both in the public's interest and in a company's own long-term interest. A company can do well by incorporating socially responsible principles into its business operations.

An important part of this perspective is the triple bottom line, which evaluates organizational performance using more than traditional economic value.

Triple Bottom Line: Economic Value + Social Value + Environmental Value

Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) Factors

Corporations use Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) factors in planning to assess and manage risks and opportunities associated with their impact on society and the environment.

ESG analysis can include examining the environmental effects of operations and supply chains, the treatment of employees and other stakeholders, and the way the organization is governed and managed. Considering these factors can help companies identify and reduce potential negative impacts, strengthen their reputation, and discover opportunities for growth and innovation.

ESG has also become increasingly important to investors. As sustainable investing has grown, investors may consider a company's ESG performance when evaluating its long-term risks, opportunities, and ability to create value.

What's the Difference Between CSR and ESG?

CSR

Corporate Social Responsibility describes a company's broader commitment to operating in an ethical and responsible manner.

It considers the organization's overall impact on society and the environment and may include activities such as philanthropy, community engagement, ethical business practices, and environmental sustainability initiatives.

Main idea: What responsibility does the corporation have to society and the environment?

ESG

Environmental, Social, and Governance refers to criteria used to assess and manage risks and opportunities related to environmental impact, stakeholder treatment, and organizational governance.

ESG is commonly used by investors and organizations when evaluating long-term sustainability and a company's ability to create lasting value.

Main idea: How can environmental, social, and governance factors affect risk, opportunity, and long-term performance?

In short: CSR focuses broadly on a company's responsibility and impact, while ESG provides a more structured way to evaluate environmental, social, and governance risks, opportunities, and long-term sustainability.
