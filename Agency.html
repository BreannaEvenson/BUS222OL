Chapter overview

This chapter introduces the next unit, Regulation in the Workplace. Its starting point is agency: the legal relationship that allows one person to act on behalf of another. 1 Agency basics: principal, agent, and control

Agency exists when an agent agrees to act on behalf of a principal, subject to the principal's control. A third party is someone outside that relationship who deals with the agent.

PrincipalAuthorizes another to act→AgentActs on the principal's behalf→Third partyDeals with the agent

Agency law commonly operates alongside contract law (who is bound by an agreement?) and tort law (who may be responsible for injuries or other wrongful acts?).

2 Types of agents and worker classification

The chapter introduces three broad categories of agents. The amount of direction and control the principal exercises is an important factor in distinguishing an employee from an independent contractor.

Employee agent

An employee authorized to act for the employer or principal. The business may direct both the work and how the work is performed.

Independent contractor agent

A separate worker or business authorized to act for a principal, generally with greater independence over the methods used to accomplish the work.

Gratuitous agent

Someone who agrees to act on a principal's behalf without compensation. Payment is not required to create an agency relationship.

The IRS looks at three groups of facts

Behavioral control

Who directs when, where, and how the work is done? What instructions or training are required?

Financial control

Who supplies the tools, pays expenses, sets payment arrangements, or bears business risk?

Relationship

Is the arrangement ongoing? Are benefits provided? Is the work a key part of the business?

Additional reading: the ABC test

Some state laws apply an ABC test to particular worker-classification questions. The details vary by jurisdiction and applicable law. In its common form, the business must establish that the worker:

  • A: Is free from direction and control in performing the work.
  • B: Performs work outside the business's usual course of business.
  • C: Is customarily engaged in an independently established business of the same nature.

Your chapter's California gig-work discussion provides historical context; don't assume one state's rule applies everywhere or that the text describes the latest litigation or exceptions.

Important distinction: Calling someone an “independent contractor” in an agreement does not, by itself, settle their legal status. Different federal and state laws may use different tests.

3 Creating and ending an agency transaction

An agency transaction typically moves through three phases. A written contract can establish agency, but the parties' words and conduct may also show the necessary consent and control.

PHASE 1Creation

The principal manifests consent for the agent to act on the principal's behalf, subject to control, and the agent agrees.

PHASE 2Performance

The agent acts within the role and authority granted; both parties carry out their duties.

PHASE 3Termination

The relationship ends through the parties' actions or, in certain circumstances, operation of law.

4 Authority, contracts, and tort liability

Whether a principal is bound by an agent's contract often depends on the agent's authority. In tort cases, the key question is often whether the wrongful act occurred within the scope of employment.

Express actual authority

The principal directly authorizes the agent to do something.

Example: “You may order $500 in supplies.”

Implied actual authority

Authority follows reasonably from the assigned task, prior dealings, or ordinary practices.

Example: A purchasing manager routinely orders ordinary supplies.

Apparent authority

The principal's conduct leads a third party reasonably to believe the agent has authority, even if actual authority is lacking.

Example: The owner presents someone as the purchasing manager to vendors.

Ratification

The principal later affirms an agent's previously unauthorized transaction, potentially making it binding.

Example: The owner knowingly approves the unauthorized purchase afterward.

AgencyWhat the third party knowsGeneral contractual consequence
Fully disclosedThe agent acts for a principal, and the principal's identity is known.An authorized agent generally does not incur personal contract liability; the principal is bound.
Partially disclosedThe third party knows an agent represents someone but does not know the principal's identity.Both the principal and agent may be liable.
UndisclosedThe third party believes the agent acts on their own behalf.The agent is liable; the undisclosed principal may also be liable under applicable law.

Tort liability: respondeat superior

Under respondeat superior, an employer may be vicariously liable for an employee's tort when it occurs within the scope of employment. The chapter distinguishes a minor work-related detour from a substantial personal frolic. It also introduces the going-and-coming rule, intentional torts, negligent hiring, and independent-contractor exceptions.

Keep the issues separate: Contract authority, vicarious tort liability, and negligent hiring are different routes to liability. The result can depend on the facts and the jurisdiction.
5 Duties, obligations, and remedies

The principal–agent relationship is fiduciary: an agent must act with particular care for the principal's interests. The chapter identifies five core duties.

Loyalty

Put the principal's interests ahead of conflicting personal interests in agency matters.

Obedience

Follow the principal's lawful directions within the scope of the agency.

Care

Use the care and skill a reasonable person would exercise in carrying out the assignment.

Disclosure

Keep the principal informed of material information relevant to the agency.

Accounting

Maintain appropriate records for money and property handled for the principal.

The principal's duties

Under applicable rules and the agreement, reimburse authorized expenses and indemnify for certain covered losses.

Possible remedies discussed in the reading include damages, indemnification, rescission, and disgorgement of profits from a loyalty breach. The applicable remedy depends on the violation and the facts.

6 Termination of an agency relationship

Agency authority can end through the parties' own actions or through operation of law. Consider both the end of actual authority and the possibility that third parties may still believe the agent is authorized.

Express acts

  • The principal or agent communicates an end to the relationship.
  • An agreed fixed term expires.
  • The agency's stated purpose is accomplished.

Operation of law

  • Destruction of essential subject matter.
  • Death or certain changes in legal capacity.
  • Some bankruptcy-related circumstances.

Specific rules and exceptions vary.

Think ahead: If a business ends an agent's authority but does not notify vendors who have dealt with the agent, what risk might remain? Consider the concept of apparent authority.
