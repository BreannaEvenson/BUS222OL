Chapter overview

This chapter examines how corporations are formed, funded, taxed, and managed. You'll also explore the responsibilities of shareholders, directors, and officers—and the legal duties that guide their decisions.

What is a corporation?

A corporation is a separate legal entity created under state law. It can hold property, enter contracts, and have obligations distinct from its owners.

Common misconception: “Corporation” doesn't necessarily mean a giant multinational company. Small businesses—even one-person businesses—may choose to incorporate.

Private versus publicly held

Privately held

Ownership interests aren't generally offered for trading to the general public on a stock exchange. These businesses can be small or large.

Publicly held

Shares can be offered to the investing public, subject to securities laws. An initial public offering (IPO) can mark the transition.

Explore additional corporate categories
  • Domestic: incorporated in the state being discussed.
  • Foreign: incorporated in another U.S. state.
  • Alien: incorporated under another country's laws.
  • Nonprofit: organized for qualifying purposes other than distributing profits to owners.
  • Public corporation: a term that can refer to a government-created corporation (different from a publicly traded business).
  • Professional: corporation organized for a licensed profession, where permitted.

From idea to corporation

1. Choose a state

Formation is governed by state law. The home state is often practical, though businesses may evaluate other states.

2. File articles

A promoter submits articles of incorporation containing required details such as name, purpose, and share information.

3. Organize

Adopt bylaws, choose directors and officers, and authorize or issue shares through required organizational actions.

4. Operate & comply

Maintain records, hold required meetings or written consents, file reports, and follow corporate formalities.

What if someone signs a contract before the corporation exists?
A promoter who signs before incorporation may be personally liable. The corporation's later adoption of the contract does not automatically release the promoter in every case; releasing that liability often requires the other contracting party's agreement (novation). Apply the governing state's law.

How do corporations raise capital?

Debt financing

Borrowed money, such as loans or bonds. Repayment is generally required; lending doesn't by itself confer ownership.

Equity financing

Selling shares representing ownership interests. Investors may gain rights connected to those shares.

Venture capital

Investment by professional investors into developing businesses, typically in exchange for an equity interest.

Public offering

Sale of securities to public investors under applicable securities regulation.

Corporations and taxation

C corporation

The corporation generally pays income tax on its earnings. If after-tax profits are distributed as dividends, shareholders may also pay tax on those dividends.

S corporation

A qualifying corporation elects federal pass-through tax treatment. Income generally passes through to shareholders, with eligibility and other tax rules.

Remember: “C” and “S” describe federal tax treatment, not two completely different state-law corporate entity forms. Eligibility and tax consequences can be complex.
What restrictions apply to an S corporation?
Generally, an S corporation must meet rules involving the number and eligible types of shareholders, stock classes, domestic status, and a valid shareholder-approved election. Some corporations are ineligible. Consult current IRS requirements for the complete list.

Limited liability isn't absolute

Corporations generally shield shareholders from personal liability for corporate debts. But a person may become personally responsible through a personal guarantee, their own wrongful acts, or certain situations where a court pierces the corporate veil.

Consider: An owner signs a business loan and also signs a personal guarantee. The corporation can't repay the loan. Does the corporation's limited-liability status automatically protect that owner's personal assets?

No. A personal guarantee can create personal repayment responsibility despite the business's separate legal identity.

Common officer roles
  • President: oversees ordinary business operations.
  • Vice president: duties depend on delegation and organizational structure.
  • Treasurer: commonly oversees financial matters.
  • Secretary: commonly maintains or certifies corporate records.

Fiduciary duties

Duty of care

Act in good faith, make reasonably informed decisions, exercise appropriate diligence and oversight, and seek the corporation's best interests.

Duty of loyalty

Put the corporation's interests ahead of conflicting personal interests and handle self-dealing conflicts appropriately.

Business judgment rule

Courts generally defer to informed, good-faith business decisions made without disabling conflicts of interest, even if the decision turns out poorly. It is not a free pass for negligence or self-dealing.

Fiduciary obligations, enforcement, and who may sue can depend on a person's role and the state law governing the corporation.

Think like a business lawyer

Scenario: Three friends form a corporation to operate a local clothing company. One provides most of the capital, one serves as director, and one manages daily operations. The director approves a contract with a company owned by their sibling without disclosing the relationship.

See Answer
Possible written answer:

The three friends may have different corporate roles. The person providing most of the capital is likely a shareholder, who owns part of the corporation. The director helps oversee corporate decisions, while the person managing daily operations may be an officer if appointed to that role.

The director's approval of a contract with a sibling's company raises a potential duty of loyalty issue because the director has a personal relationship that could influence the decision. The duty of care is also relevant: the director should become reasonably informed and evaluate whether the contract serves the corporation's interests.

Before deciding whether a fiduciary duty was breached, I would ask: Did the director disclose the family relationship and any financial interest? Was the director involved in voting on the contract? Did disinterested directors or shareholders approve it after full disclosure? Were other vendors considered, and were the terms fair to the corporation? A family relationship creates a potential conflict, but it does not by itself establish that a legal duty was breached; the governing state's corporate law and the facts matter.
