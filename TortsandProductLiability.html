Part 1

Tort Law Foundations

A tort is a civil wrong. A person or business, the tortfeasor, acts or fails to act, and that tortious conduct causes another party to suffer a loss.

Where the law comes from

Tort law is governed primarily by state common law, with guidance from the Restatement of Torts.

Products liability rules

Product-related claims may be governed by state common law or by state statutes that impose liability for injuries caused by products.

Keep the systems separate: tort cases generally concern civil responsibility and damages. They are not criminal prosecutions, even when the underlying conduct is serious.

Part 2

Intentional Business-Related Torts

Intentional torts begin with deliberate conduct. Open each topic to see the claim, its required facts, and the business context.

Defamation: libel, slander, and trade libel

Defamation involves a false statement about an identifiable person, company, or product that is communicated to a third party and causes harm. Libel is written; slander is spoken.

  • Public figures: They generally must also prove actual malice—knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth.
  • Trade libel/product disparagement: A competitor makes a false statement that disparages another company's product and causes economic harm.
  • Truth: An absolute defense. A truthful statement is not defamation.
  • Absolute privilege: Protects certain statements in official settings, such as judicial proceedings and legislative activity.
  • Qualified privilege: May protect good-faith statements made without malice, including certain employment references or media reports.
Online content providers and third-party posts

Traditional defamation law may impose liability on a publisher, while a distributor is generally treated differently. Early internet cases disagreed about whether an online service became a publisher by exercising editorial control.

Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act of 1996 created a broad federal rule that generally prevents an interactive computer service from being treated as the publisher or speaker of information supplied by another content provider. The chapter traces this development through Cubby v. CompuServe, Stratton Oakmont v. Prodigy, and Blumenthal v. Drudge/AOL.

Fraudulent misrepresentation

A claimant generally must show an intentional misrepresentation of a material fact, reasonable reliance on that fact, and damages caused by the reliance. A careless but not intentional false statement may support a negligent-misrepresentation theory.

False imprisonment and the merchant's defense

False imprisonment is the intentional, unlawful restraint of another person. A merchant may have a defense when it temporarily detains a suspected shoplifter in a reasonable manner and for a reasonable amount of time.

Privacy torts

Public disclosure

Highly offensive disclosure of private facts that are not of legitimate public concern.

Misappropriation

Commercial use of another person's name, image, voice, or likeness without permission.

False light

Publication that seriously misleads or distorts the truth about a person, with knowledge of or reckless disregard for falsity.

Interference with business relationships

Interference with an existing contract may occur when a defendant knows of a contract, actively and improperly induces a party to break it, and causes identifiable damages.

Interference with prospective advantage protects some expected business relationships from highly anticompetitive or independently wrongful conduct. Merely offering a customer a better price is ordinarily competition—not tortious interference.

Part 3

Negligence: A Five-Element Chain

Negligence applies when a party unintentionally causes harm by failing to act reasonably. The injured party must establish every element.

Duty+Breach+Cause in fact+Proximate cause+Actual damages

Special Duty and Breach Rules

No general duty to rescue

A person is ordinarily not liable simply for failing to act. A duty may arise from a special relationship, such as common carrier–passenger, innkeeper–guest, employer–employee, school–student, landlord–tenant, or business–visitor.

Assumption of duty

A person who voluntarily begins helping must use reasonable care while providing that assistance.

Negligence per se

Violation of a safety statute can create a strong presumption of breach when the statute was intended to prevent the kind of harm that occurred.

Res ipsa loquitur

“The thing speaks for itself.” Certain facts may support an inference of negligence even when the injured party cannot show exactly what the defendant did wrong.

Defenses to Negligence

Comparative negligence

If the injured party also acted negligently, responsibility is assigned by percentage and recovery may be reduced under the state's rule.

Assumption of the risk

The injured party knew of a substantial, apparent risk and voluntarily chose to proceed with the dangerous activity.

Part 4

Strict Liability

Strict liability may apply even when the defendant did not intend harm and used reasonable care. The doctrine focuses on who should bear the cost of certain unusually dangerous risks.

Two major settings: abnormally dangerous activities and defective products.

Is the Activity Abnormally Dangerous?

Courts weigh a group of factors rather than relying on one automatic rule.

1. High degree of riskHow likely is harm?
2. Serious potential harmHow great could the damage be?
3. Care cannot remove riskDoes danger remain despite reasonable care?
4. Uncommon activityIs it outside ordinary community usage?
5. LocationIs the activity inappropriate where it occurs?
6. Community valueDo the benefits outweigh the dangerous attributes?

Choose the most likely liability category

Part 5

Products Liability

Products liability is the potential liability of a manufacturer, retailer, or other seller when a product defect causes harm to a consumer.

Three Theories of Recovery

Focus: the seller's conduct

A party that negligently manufactures or handles a product may be liable for injuries proximately caused by that carelessness. MacPherson v. Buick helped establish that manufacturers may owe duties beyond the immediate purchaser.

Focus: promises about the product

Warranty theory protects buyers when a product fails to satisfy an express promise or an implied warranty concerning its quality or fitness.

Focus: the defective product

Under Restatement §402A, a commercial seller may be strictly liable when a product reaches the user in a defective condition that is unreasonably dangerous and the defect causes injury.

What Counts as a Defect?

A product must be defective and create danger beyond a reasonable consumer's expectations.

Design defect

The product line is designed in an unreasonably dangerous way, and a feasible alternative design may have reduced or avoided the foreseeable risk.

Manufacturing defect

A mistake during production causes one unit or batch to depart from its intended design.

Inadequate warning

A product carries a nonobvious risk, but the warnings or instructions do not reasonably communicate it.

What Must Connect?

Defect+Causation+Actual injury=Potential liability

Seller's Defenses

Substantial change

The product was materially altered after leaving the seller and before reaching the user.

Assumption of the risk

The user knew or should have known of the danger and deliberately continued for the user's own benefit.

Product misuse

The user handled the product in a way an ordinarily prudent person would not, especially when that use was not reasonably foreseeable.

Defect detective

Identify the strongest defect category
