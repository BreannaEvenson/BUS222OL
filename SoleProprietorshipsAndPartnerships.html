What matters when choosing an entity?

There is no single best structure for every business. Owners should consider five dimensions when deciding how to organize their business.

Formation

How easily is the business created? Are government filings or formal documents required?

02

Can creditors pursue the owner's personal assets for business debts?

03

How can the business raise money—through loans, personal funds, or ownership interests?

04

Taxation

Who reports and pays taxes on business income?

05

Management & operations

Who makes decisions, manages activities, and can legally act for the business?

3

Understanding partnership law

A general partnership can exist when people act as co-owners of a business for profit, even without a written agreement or an explicit intention to become partners. Simply calling a relationship a partnership does not necessarily make it one. A joint venture is a collaboration limited in duration and governed by similar legal principles.

Substance over form: The parties' real conduct and business relationship matter more than the label they use.

Explore the important rules

General partnership: default rules and fiduciary duties
  • In the chapter's framework, the Revised Uniform Partnership Act (RUPA) supplies default rules when the partners have no agreement.
  • Partners generally share profits and losses equally unless they agree otherwise.
  • A partner is not automatically entitled to a separate salary for work performed.
  • Partners owe fiduciary duties of loyalty and care and an obligation of good faith.
  • Joint and several liability may place each partner's personal assets at risk for the partnership's full obligations, not merely that partner's ownership percentage.
What is joint and several liability?

Joint means the partners are responsible together. Several means an individual partner may also be pursued for the full amount. For example, with a $60,000 business debt, a creditor may seek the entire amount from one general partner even if that person owns only 25% of the partnership. Rights to reimbursement between partners are a separate issue.

Limited partnership: two types of partners

General partner

  • Manages daily operations
  • May bind the partnership
  • Personally liable for partnership obligations

Limited partner

  • Primarily contributes capital or property
  • Typically does not handle daily management
  • Normally risks the value of their contribution rather than all personal assets
  • Has rights to partnership information

The text emphasizes that courts consider conduct, not just labels, when assessing limited partner status. The chapter also identifies illegal or negligent conduct as a potential source of personal liability.

Limited partnership: formation, allocation, and special purposes
  • A general partner files a certificate of limited partnership with the appropriate state authority.
  • The chapter describes the Revised Uniform Limited Partnership Act (RULPA) as providing default rules where no agreement governs.
  • Without a different agreement, profits and losses are allocated in proportion to the value of partners' unreturned contributions.
  • Family limited partnerships may be used in estate planning and intergenerational asset transfers.
Capitalization and pass-through taxation

Partnerships commonly raise funds using debt (borrowing) or equity (ownership contributions or interests). They do not sell partnership ownership through public stock markets. Under pass-through treatment, partners generally report allocated business income on their tax returns, while the partnership files an informational return.

What happens when a partner leaves?

Dissociation

A partner separates from a general partnership. The business does not automatically dissolve if the remaining partners continue it.

Rightful: Examples discussed in the chapter include voluntary departure, expulsion, incapacity, or death. The chapter states the departing partner generally is not responsible for later obligations.

Wrongful: A departure that violates an agreement or agreed term may create liability for damages caused by leaving.

Dissolution and winding up

Dissolution initiates the process of ending the partnership. During winding up, the business pays debts and distributes or liquidates remaining assets before termination.

Other events described in the chapter include the end of an agreed term, a court order, or unanimous agreement.

Limited partnership withdrawal

  • Under the chapter's RULPA discussion, a general partner may withdraw without dissolution if at least one general partner remains and all partners agree in writing to continue.
  • If withdrawal does not dissolve the LP, the chapter describes payment of the departing partner's fair market interest within a reasonable time.
  • The chapter describes a restrictive default rule for limited partners wishing to withdraw before the agreed termination date.

Franchises: a method, not an entity

Franchising is a contractual method of operating a business, not an additional category of legal entity. A franchisor allows a franchisee to use its business model, trademarks, and expertise under a franchise agreement.

Franchisor

The company granting rights to use its brand, model, and operating systems.

Franchisee

The operator paying for those rights and agreeing to follow the contract's requirements.

What's typically in a franchise agreement?

  • Agreement term
  • Upfront fees and payment terms
  • Territory rights
  • Required purchasing or investment
  • Training, support, and advertising
  • Operational standards
  • Royalties and ongoing fees
  • Termination and cancellation
Who regulates franchising?

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) regulates franchise disclosure at the federal level so prospective franchisees receive important information before investing. States may impose additional franchise registration and disclosure requirements.

Remember: A franchisee still needs a legal business structure. “Franchise” tells you how the business operates under a brand agreement, not whether its legal form is a sole proprietorship or partnership (or another entity).
