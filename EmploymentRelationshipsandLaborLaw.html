01 · Historical context

Why employment rules developed

Industrial expansion

In the late 1800s, work increasingly shifted from farms to factories. Many immigrant workers and displaced farmers faced long hours, low pay, and hazardous conditions.

Labor movement and legislation

Organized workers pushed for protections. Federal and state laws developed around wages, child labor, workplace safety, and union activity.

Big idea: Employment regulation concerns employee well-being, while labor law governs workers’ rights to organize and operate labor unions.

02 · The employment relationship

Employment at will and its limits

Under the general at-will rule, an employer may terminate an employee with or without advance notice and with or without cause, subject to exceptions.

Express contract

An express contractual relationship is an exception to the at-will rule.

Collective bargaining agreement

A collective bargaining agreement may prescribe a process the employer must use before terminating an employee.

Implied contract

An employer’s manual, bulletin, or oral promise may create a reasonable belief that an employee is protected from termination without cause.

Statutory protection

Federal and state statutes, including antidiscrimination laws, may displace the at-will rule.

Explore: public policy exception

This narrow exception places public welfare ahead of an employer’s rights. Examples include refusing to commit an illegal act, exercising a legal right, or performing an important act. Courts have been reluctant to expand the exception, as illustrated by Bammert v. Don’s Super-Valu, Inc.

Explore: good faith and fair dealing

A minority of states recognize this exception, protecting employees from termination without just cause or in bad faith or with malicious intent. Consider the chapter’s example: Whiteside is fired two weeks before a large sale closes and is denied a commission.

Explore: whistleblower protection

Federal and state statutes generally protect workers who report an employer’s violation of a law or standard to authorities. An employer may still terminate a whistle-blower for an independent reason unrelated to the report. The chapter identifies the Whistleblower Protection Act of 1989 for federal employees and employees of government contractors.

03 · Worker protections

Employment regulation

Federal law often works in tandem with state law on wages, overtime, child labor, sudden job loss, workplace injuries and safety, and medical leave.

Wages, hours, exemptions, and child labor

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), passed in 1938, addresses minimum wage, a standard 40-hour workweek, overtime pay, and restrictions on child labor. The chapter lists the federal minimum wage as $7.25 and explains that states may set a higher rate. Overtime is generally calculated at time and a half for hours above 40 in a seven-day workweek.

The Portal to Portal Act addresses travel to and from work and certain activities before or after the workday. The chapter describes exemptions for certain executive, administrative, and professional employees and notes that misclassification may have serious consequences. Federal child labor rules restrict work by minors; some states also regulate rest and meal periods, paydays, and prevailing wages.

Retirement, Social Security, and health care

Employers may offer pensions or retirement savings plans. When they establish retirement benefits, ERISA imposes rules about disclosures and transparency. The Social Security Act of 1935 provides retirement income and other benefits funded through employment taxes.

The chapter also discusses HIPAA protections for medical information and COBRA continuation of coverage after termination, for which the employer need not pay. It identifies the Health Care and Education Reconciliation Act of 2010 as part of the overhaul of the U.S. health care system.

Sudden job loss and WARN

The chapter describes the Federal Unemployment Tax Act (FUTA) of 1935 as part of the system that supports payments to workers who lose employment through no fault of their own. The WARN Act addresses advance written notice of certain facility closings and mass layoffs by employers with 100 or more employees.

Workers’ compensation and workplace safety

State workers’ compensation laws provide partial payment for qualifying injuries in the course of employment, generally in exchange for giving up a negligence suit against the employer. The chapter discusses accidental injury, course of employment, possible defenses, and exceptions involving intentional or reckless employer conduct.

The Occupational Safety and Health Act, passed in 1970, addresses national safety standards, hazard warnings, records and reporting, and employers’ general duty to maintain reasonably safe workplaces. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration administers and enforces the law. The chapter describes a limited employee right to leave a hazardous workplace condition in Whirlpool v. Marshall.

Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA)

The chapter describes the FMLA as providing eligible employees up to 12 weeks of unpaid leave for family medical matters at covered employers with 50 or more employees. It discusses continued health benefits, protection against adverse job action, return to the same or a similar job at the same rate of pay, and a limited reinstatement exception for certain key employees.

04 · Boundaries at work

Employee privacy

The chapter describes employee privacy at work as generally very limited, with some statutory protections.

Work systems

The chapter states that activities on an employer’s computer system are generally subject to employer monitoring, partly to manage employer liability.

Calls and messages

The Electronic Communications Privacy Act restricts monitoring personal calls or accessing office voicemail without consent, subject to an ordinary-course-of-business exception.

Drug and alcohol tests

State statutes primarily govern workplace drug and alcohol tests and vary considerably. An applicant or employee with a history of substance use disorder who is not currently in active addiction may be protected under the ADA.

Polygraphs

The Employee Polygraph Protection Act prohibits most private-sector employers from requiring a polygraph test as a condition of employment.

05 · Collective action

Labor unions and collective bargaining

The National Labor Relations Act (NLRA) protects workers’ rights to organize and engage in collective bargaining. The National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) administers and enforces it. The chapter describes coverage for employers whose business involves interstate commerce.

Three major laws

NLRA: organizing, collective bargaining, and unfair labor practices. Taft–Hartley Act (1947): addresses compulsory union membership as a condition of employment. Landrum–Griffin Act (1959): establishes reporting and checks intended to prevent fraud and corruption among union officials.

One route to union representation

1

Show interest: Employees discuss organizing and sign authorization cards.

2

Petition: At least 30% of employees in the proposed bargaining unit sign authorization cards.

3

Election: Eligible employees vote following NLRB procedures.

4

Certification: A legally sound election with a simple majority of pro-union votes leads to certification.

The chapter also describes efforts beginning in 2007 to amend the NLRA through the proposed Employee Free Choice Act.

Collective bargaining and grievances

Both parties must engage in good faith negotiations. A grievance challenges an employer’s treatment of union members; the union generally controls whether to invoke arbitration under the agreement.

Strikes, picketing, boycotts, and lockouts

The chapter describes strikes, picketing, and boycotts as ways workers may pressure an employer during bargaining. It distinguishes economic strikes from unfair-labor-practice strikes in discussing reinstatement. It also discusses illegal conduct such as violent picketing, wildcat and sit-in strikes, certain strikes during cooling-off periods, and secondary boycotts. Employers may use lockouts for legitimate business reasons and hire replacement workers during a strike.

Case study · Amazon Staten Island

Workers at an Amazon warehouse in Staten Island voted to unionize on April 1, 2022. The chapter describes their concerns about safety, pay, sick days, job security, breaks, and transportation; their use of social media and crowdfunding; and Amazon’s opposition and challenge to the election.
