Employment Discrimination

Chapter Overview

Continuing this unit’s examination of regulation of employment relationships, this chapter covers employment discrimination law, with primary coverage of the federal antidiscrimination statutory scheme. It explores the role of protected classes, examines theories of discrimination, and considers employer defenses. It also examines the role of the EEOC and the process for filing a claim, and introduces differences between state and federal antidiscrimination statutes.

1. Definitions, Sources of Law, and Statutory Origins

Federal and state statutes prohibit employment discrimination in hiring, employee treatment, work schedules, working conditions, assignments, and disciplinary action.

Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC): The administrative agency responsible for carrying out federal workplace antidiscrimination laws through rulemaking, investigation, and enforcement. The EEOC pursues only a fraction of employee claims.

2. Federal Workplace Antidiscrimination Statutes

  • Title VII of the Civil Rights Act
  • Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA)
  • Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)

3. Title VII

Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits workplace discrimination based on membership in a protected class. It covers private sector employers, labor unions, employment agencies, state and local governments, and most federal government employers.

Protected Classes

Protection applies when discrimination is based on membership in a protected class. A plaintiff does not need to be a minority within the protected class to be covered.

Discrimination Key Ideas

Disparate treatment
Intentional discrimination: an employer treats an employee differently based on membership in a protected class.
Mixed motives
Legitimate motives are mixed with illegitimate motives in an employment decision.
Disparate impact
A facially neutral practice adversely affects members of a protected class. A plaintiff must show statistically significant differences that adversely affect protected class members.

The McDonnell Douglas Burden-Shifting Analysis

  1. The plaintiff establishes a prima facie case.
  2. The employer presents a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for its action.
  3. The individual shows that the employer’s stated reason was not the actual reason for its action.

Prima facie case: Evidence sufficient to support a claim unless disproved or rebutted by the opposing party. The appropriate elements are adjusted for the type of employment action involved.

Preponderance of the evidence: More likely than not; a lower standard than clear and convincing evidence.

Sexual Harassment

Sexual harassment includes unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favors, and other verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature.

  • Quid pro quo: The conduct is tied to explicit or implicit conditions of employment or used as a basis for employment decisions.
  • Hostile work environment: The chapter examines harassment that interferes with work performance or creates an offensive work environment.
  • Same-sex harassment: Title VII’s protections apply regardless of the gender of the victim or harasser, as recognized in Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services, Inc.
  • Supervisor harassment: An employer is strictly liable when supervisor harassment culminates in a tangible employment action, such as termination or transfer to a less desirable job.

The #MeToo movement helped educate society about hurdles faced by people accusing employers of workplace sexual harassment.

Remedies

Title VII remedies include injunctions, reinstatement, compensatory damages, retroactive promotions, and remedial actions by the employer.

4. Age Discrimination in Employment Act

The ADEA prohibits age discrimination against employees who are 40 years old or older.

The outline describes a claim involving satisfactory job performance, an adverse job action, and better treatment of someone substantially younger.

After an employee establishes a prima facie case, the employer presents a nondiscriminatory reason. The employee then seeks to show that the reason is pretextual and that age discrimination was the real reason.

Disparate impact claims are also recognized under the ADEA.

5. Americans with Disabilities Act

The ADA seeks to eliminate discriminatory employment practices against individuals with disabilities. Employers with 15 or more employees must provide reasonable accommodations that allow an employee with a disability to perform essential job functions, unless the accommodation causes an undue hardship.

Disability and Coverage

The ADA defines disability in terms of physical or mental impairments that substantially limit major life activities.

The ADA Amendments Act of 2008 expanded protections and directed courts to interpret disability broadly.

Regarded as: An employee may be protected when the employer regards the employee as having an impairment, even if the impairment does not actually meet the definition of a disability.

Reasonable Accommodations

  • Making existing facilities accessible
  • Job restructuring
  • Part-time or modified work schedules
  • Reassignment to vacant positions
  • Providing readers or interpreters

Undue hardship: Significant difficulty or expense considered in light of the employer’s resources, workforce, expenses, and operations.

6. Equal Pay Act

The Equal Pay Act makes it illegal to pay unequal wages to men and women performing substantially equal work, unless the difference is based on a factor other than gender.

The Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act of 2009 seeks to ensure that employees have the opportunity to challenge discriminatory paychecks.

7. Procedures for Asserting a Claim

The chapter examines filing a complaint with the EEOC, the agency’s investigation, conciliation negotiations intended to settle a case, and the EEOC’s decision whether to file suit on an employee’s behalf. It also considers the role of a right-to-sue letter in pursuing a lawsuit.

8. Employer Defenses

Each antidiscrimination statute has its own legally recognized employer defenses. Topics examined in the chapter include:

  • Business necessity
  • Faragher/Ellerth defense
  • Bona fide occupational qualification (BFOQ): Certain classifications based on religion, gender, or national origin may be reasonably necessary to a business’s normal operation, such as employment of clergy.
  • Seniority: Employment decisions made in good faith under an established, objective seniority system.
  • Employee misconduct

9. Affirmative Action Programs

Outreach to recruit minority applicants, special training programs, and reevaluation of the effects of selection criteria to increase diversity in the workplace.

10. State Antidiscrimination Statutes

States have their own antidiscrimination statutes, often modeled after federal statutes, and their own agencies responsible for enforcement and adjudication.

State laws may differ from federal laws by covering more employers, including smaller businesses, and by extending protection to additional protected classes.
