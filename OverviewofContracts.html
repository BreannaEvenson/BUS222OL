Definitions and Categories of Contracts

A contract is a promise or a set of promises enforceable by law.

Categories of Contracts

Written Oral

Any agreement, oral or written, may result in a binding contract so long as it meets certain legal requirements.

Bilateral Unilateral

A bilateral contract involves two promises and two performances.

A unilateral contract involves one promise, followed by one performance, which then triggers a second performance from the offeror.

Express Implied Quasi-Contract

An express contract is created when the parties expressly agree on the promises and performances.

An implied contract is one in which the agreement is reached through the parties' actions rather than their words.

A quasi-contract may be enforced when one party suffers a loss as a result of another party's unjust enrichment.

Valid Void

When a contract contains the necessary legal elements, it is a valid contract.

Void contracts are agreements that were not formed in conformance with the law from the outset and, therefore, cannot be enforced by either party.

Voidable Unenforceable

A voidable contract is one in which a valid contract has been formed, but one party has the option to disaffirm or enforce it.

An unenforceable contract otherwise meets the requirements of a binding agreement but is subject to a legal defense that prevents enforcement.

Sources of Contract Law

In general, contracts for services or real estate are governed by state common law.

Contracts for goods or products are generally governed by state statutory law based on Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC).

Contracts involving terms for both goods and services are known as hybrid contracts. In a hybrid contract, the source of law is established by determining the predominant thrust of the contract's subject matter.

Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA)

The Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA) is a procedural model law that applies to transactions when the parties to a contract agree to use electronic commerce for the transaction.

UETA elevates electronic signatures and electronic records to the same legal status generally accorded to traditional signatures and paper records.

Overview of a Contract Transaction

Formation of the Contract

The formation of a contract requires mutual assent, whereby the parties reach an agreement using a combination of offer and acceptance.

Formation of a valid contract also requires that the agreement be supported by consideration, that the parties entering into the agreement have capacity, and that the subject matter and performance of the contract be legal and consistent with public policy.

In order for the contract to be enforceable, the agreement must also be the product of genuine assent. In some cases, certain terms must also be in writing as required by the statute of frauds.

Mutual Assent Part 1: Offer

An offer is a promise or commitment to do, or refrain from doing, a specified activity in exchange for the offeree's counter-promise or performance.

In order for an offer to have legal effect, the offeror must have an objective intent to contract when making the offer. The question is whether, given the language and circumstances of the offer, a reasonable person in the position of the offeree would conclude that the offer was an objective manifestation of serious intent to contract.

Under modern case law, the importance of the parties' intention—or lack of intention—to form a contract depends largely on the context of the agreement.

Advertisements as an Offer

Advertisements are generally not considered offers. Instead, they are usually invitations for consumers to make an offer to the seller of goods or services.

However, certain advertisements can be considered an offer when the advertisement is sufficiently specific to constitute a unilateral contract.

Mutual Assent Part 2: Acceptance

Acceptance is the offeree's expression of agreement to the terms of the offer. Acceptance may occur so long as the offer is still in force.

Events Terminating the Power of Acceptance

An offer may be terminated by the action of the parties in one of three primary ways:

  • Revocation
  • Rejection
  • Counteroffer

An offer may also terminate through operation of law.

Revocation: The offeror withdraws the offer by communicating the revocation to the offeree before acceptance. Most states follow the rule that revocation is effective only upon receipt by the offeree or the offeree's agent.

Certain offers may be temporarily or permanently irrevocable.

  • Option Contract: The offeror agrees to hold an offer open for a certain period of time in exchange for consideration.
  • Partial Performance and Detrimental Reliance: An offer may become temporarily irrevocable when the offeree takes action in reasonable reliance on the offer before formation, such as beginning performance or making preparations based on the offer.

Rejection and Counteroffer: An offer terminates once the offeree rejects the offer or makes a counteroffer by rejecting the original offer and proposing new terms.

The mirror image rule requires the offeree's acceptance to match the original offer. A deviation from the original offer generally constitutes a rejection and counteroffer.

Termination by Operation of Law

An offer may also terminate because of certain events, including:

  • Lapse of time
  • Death
  • Incapacity
  • Destruction of the subject matter

Lapse of Time: An offer expires after the deadline specified by the offeror, or after a reasonable time if no deadline was specified.

Death, Incapacity, or Destruction: If the offeror or offeree dies or becomes incapacitated, or if the subject matter of the contract is destroyed before acceptance, the offer is generally terminated by operation of law.

When Acceptance Is Effective: The Mailbox Rule

Under the mailbox rule, the acceptance of an offer is generally effective upon dispatch when sent using a commercially reasonable manner.

Mutual Assent

Silence as Acceptance

Under most circumstances, acceptance cannot be imposed or inferred from an offeree's silence after receiving an offer.

Courts generally will not enforce a contract in which the offer states that failure to respond will be considered acceptance.

An exception may occur when the parties previously agreed that silence would act as acceptance.

Insufficient Agreement

In some cases, the parties may satisfy the basic requirements of offer and acceptance but the agreement may still lack mutual assent and therefore be insufficient to constitute a properly formed contract.

Indefinite Terms: For an offer to be valid, the parties must generally reach mutual assent concerning the essential terms of the agreement, including the parties, subject matter, time, and consideration.

Courts sometimes supply missing terms when a term may reasonably be implied or established through the parties' prior course of dealing.

Agreements to Agree: When parties leave an essential term unresolved with the intent to agree later, a court may in some circumstances supply the missing term if the parties demonstrated an intention to make a binding contract.

Mistake

A mistake in contract law is an erroneous belief that is not in accord with the existing facts.

A mutual mistake may provide a basis for canceling a contract when both parties share an erroneous belief concerning a basic assumption on which the contract was made.

Courts generally do not treat market conditions or a party's financial ability as the type of mistake that permits a contract to be avoided.

A unilateral mistake occurs when only one party has an erroneous belief concerning a basic assumption of the agreement. It generally does not provide a basis for avoiding the contract unless the nonmistaken party had reason to know of the mistake or caused the mistake.

Consideration

For a binding contract to exist, the agreement must generally be supported by consideration.

Consideration includes:

  • Legal detriment
  • A bargained-for exchange
Capacity

Courts generally enforce contracts only when each party had the legal capacity to enter into the agreement.

Minors: Until a person reaches the age of majority, generally 18, a contract entered into by the minor is typically voidable at the minor's option.

A contract made by a minor is not automatically void. The minor may ordinarily choose to avoid the contract or enforce the contract.

One important exception to the minor-capacity rule involves contracts for necessities.

Mental Incapacity: A person may lack capacity because of mental incapacity if:

  • The person is unable to understand the nature and consequences of the contract; or
  • The person is unable to act in a reasonable manner in relation to the transaction and the other party has reason to know of the person's condition.

In most states, contracts made by an incompetent person are generally voidable and may sometimes be ratified if that person regains capacity or through an appropriately appointed guardian.
