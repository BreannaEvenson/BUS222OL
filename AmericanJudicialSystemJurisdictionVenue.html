Overview

The chapter provides students with a wide-ranging perspective of the structure and function of American courts. Lawsuits are a reality in daily business operations and business owners and managers should be prepared with a fundamental knowledge of the system. The chapter begins with an explanation of how federal and state courts operate in tandem, examines how law is developed via appellate courts, and concludes with coverage on jurisdiction and venue with consideration of special jurisdiction issues in cyberspace.

Role and Structure of the Judiciary

The judiciary is a collection of federal and state courts existing primarily to adjudicate disputes and charged with the responsibility of judicial review. State courts adjudicate matters dealing primarily with cases arising from state statutory, common, or state constitutional law while federal courts are concerned with national laws, federal constitutional issues, and other cases that are outside the purview of state courts.

State Courts

All states have two types of courts: state trial courts and state appellate courts. The majority of court cases in the United States are filed within state courts.

State Trial Courts

Where parties present their cases and evidence.

  • State trial courts have either general authority, often organized into geographic districts, or limited authority, confined to a particular type of dispute.
  • For minor matters and cases with a relatively low dollar value, typically less than $10,000, states provide local courts.
  • Trial courts are often divided into courts hearing civil matters and courts hearing criminal matters.

State Appellate Courts

Primarily concerned with reviewing the decisions of trial courts.

  • Only appellate courts are considered courts of authority that have the right to set precedent.
  • States vary in how state trial court judges are selected, including general elections, appointment, or a combination.
  • Appellate courts assess lower court rulings by reviewing transcripts and rulings, reading briefs, and sometimes allowing attorneys to participate in oral argument.

Federal Courts

The federal court system consists primarily of U.S. District Courts, U.S. Courts of Appeals, and the U.S. Supreme Court.

U.S. District Courts

Serve the same trial function as state trial courts, but hear federal matters and certain disputes involving parties from different states when jurisdictional requirements are met.

Circuit Courts of Appeals

The thirteen U.S. Courts of Appeals review decisions of federal district courts.

  • The circuits are divided geographically.
  • They are intermediate appellate courts of precedent in the federal system.

U.S. Supreme Court

The nine justices of the Supreme Court are the ultimate arbiters of federal law. The Court has both original and appellate jurisdiction and can finalize a legal decision on a case within its authority.

The odds of reaching this level are slim. Many Supreme Court cases involve conflicts among federal circuits or particularly significant and complex legal issues.

All federal judges are selected through the appointment process.

How the Law Develops

State trial courts rule on certain points of state law and render a decision that is binding on the parties, but no one else. Only when an appellate court affirms or reverses a decision can that point of law become authority applied to future cases.

Jurisdiction's origins lie in the Constitution, including the Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments, while appellate courts and legislatures have shaped the framework modern courts use to analyze jurisdiction questions.

A court must satisfy a two-part jurisdiction analysis:

1

Subject Matter Jurisdiction

Does this court have authority over the type of dispute involved?

2

Personal Jurisdiction

Does this court have authority over the parties involved?

Jurisdiction and Venue

Jurisdiction is a court's authority to decide a particular case based on (1) who the parties are and (2) the subject matter of the dispute.

Venue is a determination of the most appropriate court location for litigating a dispute.

Business Strategy Connection: The cost-benefit analysis involving jurisdictional restrictions may affect managerial decision-making when a company or individual is considering whether and where to file a lawsuit.

Internet and Email Jurisdiction

Courts tend to diverge over what constitutes sufficient minimum contacts with a forum state to establish personal jurisdiction over an out-of-state defendant.

Some courts have ruled that a physical presence in the forum state is not necessary, as illustrated by Costar Realty Information v. Meissner.

Other courts have concluded that e-mail communications alone may be insufficient to establish personal jurisdiction, as illustrated by Engineers Corp. v. Geometric Ltd.

International Jurisdiction in the Internet Age

Country of Origin Standard

In international Web transactions, courts generally look to the location of a Web site's servers when determining jurisdiction over disputes arising from use of the site.

Other Theories of Jurisdiction in Electronic Commerce

Internet activity can create difficult jurisdiction questions across national borders. In 2002, Australia's highest court concluded that American-based publisher Dow Jones could be sued in Australia for defamation based on an article appearing on its Web site, even though Dow Jones had no physical presence in Australia and its Web site infrastructure was located in New Jersey.

Key takeaway: Conducting business or publishing information online can expose a company to legal systems far beyond the location of its physical offices.
